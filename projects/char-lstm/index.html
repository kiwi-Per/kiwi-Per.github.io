JAX / Flax, Team project

Predicting text one character at a time

Two language models (an LSTM and a decoder-only transformer) written from scratch in JAX/Flax and trained on 90 million characters of Wikipedia. Then the question: does attention actually beat recurrence at this scale? A visit to the MIT Museum later sent me back to compare everything against Claude Shannon's 1951 experiments on the same problem.

In short
  • The transformer predicts the next character correctly 67.94% of the time. The LSTM reaches 66.38% with almost twice the parameters.
  • Most errors happen at the first letter of a word, where the model has to guess the word itself.
  • With a few letters of context, the LSTM guesses as well as the people in Claude Shannon's 1951 experiment. With 99 letters, people are well ahead.
  • You can type into the trained model and play the guessing game against it on this page.

Tuning with Bayesian optimisation

Instead of grid search, hyperparameters were tuned with Optuna's Tree-structured Parzen Estimator, which models which settings tend to produce good losses and samples there next. For the transformer, a successive-halving pruner killed weak trials early: 57 of 75 were stopped after a few hundred steps, so the compute went to the promising configurations.

Why we got these results

After the project I went back and asked what the numbers actually say about the data. Text8 itself couldn't be downloaded here, so the breakdowns below run the trained LSTM on public-domain books cleaned into the same 27-character format. Absolute accuracy is lower on that text (59.8%) because it isn't Wikipedia, but the patterns are what matter.

01Most of the uncertainty is about which word comes next

Language is not equally predictable everywhere. Once a word has started, the remaining letters are heavily constrained by spelling. The space that ends a word is right 92% of the time. The first letter of a new word is a genuine choice among thousands of possible words, and the model gets it right only 21% of the time. First letters are 19% of all characters but cause 39% of the total loss, and the first two letters together cause 60%.

02Most of the useful context is the last few words

I measured accuracy against how much text the LSTM had seen so far, and compared it with simple n-gram models (count tables of what follows each short sequence of characters). Accuracy climbs fast through the first 16 characters, about three words, then nearly flattens: everything from character 17 out to 512 adds only about one more percentage point.

This is why the transformer's advantage is small. Attention is strongest at retrieving long-range information, but at character level on encyclopedic text there is little long-range signal left once you know the current word and the one before it. An LSTM's recurrent state carries that short window well.

03Both models were limited by size, not by overfitting

With 78.75M training characters and only 4.8M (transformer) or 9.0M (LSTM) parameters, there is far more data than capacity. The transformer saw each character about 12 times and the LSTM 10 times. The curves above confirm it: the transformer's final train and validation losses are 1.005 vs 1.006, and the LSTM's validation loss was still falling at epoch 10. That's also why Bayesian optimisation picked almost no dropout (0.3% and 2.8%): regularisation doesn't help a model that isn't overfitting.

Implication: the next gains would come from a bigger model or longer training, not from tuning. Also worth noting: the transformer beat an LSTM with nearly twice its parameters.

04How much of the 1.56-point gap is real?

With 5M test characters, random sampling noise in each accuracy is tiny (hundredths of a percentage point). The bigger caveats are elsewhere:

  • Different evaluation windows. The transformer was scored over 192-character windows and the LSTM over 128, so on average the transformer had more context. On the book text, re-scoring the LSTM with 192-character windows raises its accuracy by about 0.5 points, so roughly a third of the gap could be the protocol, not the architecture.
  • One training run each. Without repeating training with different random seeds, we don't know how much the results vary from run to run.
  • Short tuning runs. Tuning ranked settings after only 800 steps, which favours settings that learn fast early (high learning rate, little dropout). That happened to suit this data-rich problem but could pick the wrong settings in a smaller one.

Fair conclusion: the transformer is modestly better and clearly more parameter-efficient, but the honest margin is closer to 1 point than 1.5.

Connecting to Shannon (1951)

How this section came about. In 2026 I visited the MIT Museum and ended up spending most of my time with Claude Shannon's work. The piece that stopped me was Theseus, a mechanical mouse he built in 1950 that finds its way through a maze. The "brain" is a bank of telephone relays under the floor that remembers the route, so on its second run the mouse goes straight to the goal. It's one of the earliest machines that learned. From there I went down the rabbit hole of his information theory, and then came across a later paper of his that felt eerily familiar: he had spent 1951 asking how well the next letter of English can be predicted from the letters before it. That is the exact task my teammates and I had trained neural networks on. So I went back to our results to see how they measure up against his.

In "Prediction and Entropy of Printed English", Claude Shannon asked how predictable English is, one letter at a time. His setup is remarkably close to this project. He reduced English to the same 27 symbols as text8 (26 letters plus space), and his "guessing game" asked people to predict the next letter after seeing the previous ones. The fraction of first guesses that were right is exactly what our models' accuracy measures, and his entropy estimates are in the same unit as our loss: bits per character.

01Counting gets you to about 3 bits; context gets you much further

Shannon first estimated entropy from letter-frequency tables (n-grams). I fitted the same kind of count models on public-domain books and, 75 years later, got almost identical numbers for the first two. Neural models then go far below what counting alone can reach.

Shannon concluded that English carries roughly one bit per letter, about 75% redundancy. The transformer's 1.48 bits implies at least 69% redundancy in text8. A model's cross-entropy can only overestimate the true entropy, so the remaining gap to Shannon's human estimate is how much the models still miss.

02Re-running the guessing game with the LSTM

I repeated Shannon's experiment with the trained LSTM in his place: pick a random point in a book, show it 0, 1, 2 … 14 or 99 letters, and check whether its top guess for the next letter is right. I used 2,000 samples per setting, where Shannon had 100. The text is literary, like his source (a biography of Thomas Jefferson).

First guess correct, by letters known

Shannon's human subjects vs the trained LSTM. The point at 0 letters known is the letter-frequency count model, since the LSTM needs at least one letter. The last point is 99 letters known (axis shortened).

With up to about four letters known, the LSTM matches people almost exactly: 28% vs 29% with one letter known, 37% vs 36% with two. That part of prediction is spelling statistics, and the model has learned them as well as a person consults them. With long context, people pull far ahead (80% vs 59%), because they use meaning: what the sentence is about and where it's going. Shannon found that errors happen most often at "the beginning of words and syllables". That is exactly the pattern in finding 01 above, where first letters cause 39% of the model's loss.

03Shannon's bounds hold, and they're wide

Shannon couldn't read a person's probabilities, only the order of their guesses. From how often the right answer was the 1st, 2nd, 3rd… guess, he derived an upper and a lower bound on the entropy. A model lets us test that method, because we know its true value. With 99 letters known, the LSTM's guess ranks give bounds of 1.35–2.35 bits, and its actual cross-entropy, 1.92, falls inside. The method works, but the interval is a full bit wide, so his 0.6–1.3 range is a loose bracket, not a measurement.

Sample size matters too. With 100 samples per column, each of Shannon's percentages carries about ±5 points of sampling error, which explains why his table is bumpy: 72% correct with 13 letters known, but 60% with 14. With 2,000 samples, the LSTM's curve is smooth (±1 point).

04The three model families, through Shannon's lens

  • N-gram count models are Shannon's FN, built literally. They need a table entry for every context, which is why he could only go to 3 letters. The number of possible contexts grows 27-fold with each extra letter.
  • The LSTM squeezes the entire history into a fixed-size memory. In principle it has unbounded context, but in practice it uses mostly the last few words (see the context chart above).
  • The transformer looks back directly at all 192 previous letters and learns which ones matter, a learned, shared-parameter version of a very long N-gram. This is the closest of the three to Shannon's human predictor, who could take in all 100 letters at once.

Shannon also observed that a perfect predictor is a perfect compressor: you only need to transmit where the right answer ranks in its guesses. The same holds for our models. Paired with arithmetic coding, a technique that turns probabilities into a compressed bit stream, the transformer would store text8 at about 1.48 bits per character, under a fifth of plain 8-bit text.
