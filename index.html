Predicting text one letter at a time

Team project. JAX and Flax.

We wrote a transformer and an LSTM from scratch and trained them on 90 million characters of Wikipedia. The transformer won by a small margin. Later I replayed Claude Shannon's 1951 letter-guessing experiment with the trained model. It matched people when it knew only a few letters, and lost to them when it knew many.

  • 67.94% vs 66.38% accuracy
  • First letters of words cause 39% of the loss
  • 99 letters known: people 80%, model 59%

Does a bigger network classify better?

Team of 4. TensorFlow and Keras.

We compared five CNNs on 23 classes of traffic signs. A small pretrained network beat one about eight times its size, and fine-tuning cut its reported test errors from 12 to 2. When I reviewed the pipeline afterwards, I found that the test set changed every epoch, so most test photos had already been used for training.

  • 5 CNNs, 23 classes
  • 12 → 2 reported test errors
  • About 2 in 3 test photos seen in training

Can price history predict next week's direction?

NUS project, in progress. Python, scikit-learn and PyTorch.

I audited 17.45 million daily records and compared seven models with strict time-based testing. The answer is barely: 53.4% against a 51.8% baseline on 2013 to 2015. Most of the project explains why, and shows that features built from prices alone are unlikely to do much better.

  • 17.45M rows, 8,507 tickers
  • 7 models, 16 notebooks
  • 60% accuracy needs 5× the signal

How I work

Every project goes through the same four stages. The last one is where I learn the most.

  1. DataLook at every row before modelling. Count the classes and find the gaps.
  2. PreparationSplit first, then transform. Fit scaling on training data only.
  3. ModelsStart with a simple baseline. Keep a complex model only if it beats it.
  4. ReviewAsk what the result would be on data the model has never seen.

About me

I'm a Data Science and Analytics undergraduate at the National University of Singapore. I enjoy learning for its own sake, especially the theory side of machine learning: why an optimiser converges, what a stochastic process assumes, and where a model's guarantees stop.

That curiosity sends me back to the sources. At the MIT Museum in 2026 I found Claude Shannon's maze-solving mouse, then his information theory, then his 1951 paper on predicting English one letter at a time. It asked the same question my team had trained neural networks on, so I went back and compared our results with his.

I haven't done an internship yet. What I want next is to see this way of working hold up outside a notebook. I speak English and Chinese natively.
